\newcommand{\PfiveArchivePrimary}{\label{sec:primary}
The confirmatory comparison pairs each alternative with truncation on the same 500 questions at 4,096 tokens. Table~\ref{tab:p5-grid} lists every cell, Table~\ref{tab:p5-primary} gives the three pre-registered contrasts, and Figure~\ref{fig:p5-arms} shows both budgets. Retrieval was judged correct on 71.0\% of the questions, summaries on 37.4\%, compression on 25.2\% and truncation on 11.4\%; truncation was 2.8 points above the no-history control (8.6\%). All three paired intervals lie above zero, and all three contrasts remained significant after Holm correction (Table~\ref{tab:p5-primary}). Of the 316 questions on which retrieval and truncation disagreed, retrieval was right on 307. Retrieval yielded the most judged-correct answers. Only its lead over truncation was tested; its leads over summaries (33.6 points) and compression (45.8 points) are descriptive, with non-overlapping Wilson intervals (Table~\ref{tab:p5-grid}). No generation or judge verdict failed in any cell.

\newcommand{\PfiveArchiveBudget}{\label{sec:budget}
At 1,024 tokens the four policies scored 39.0\% (retrieval), 15.0\% (summaries), 12.4\% (compression) and 10.0\% (truncation), the same order of point estimates as at 4,096 tokens, at lower levels. Against truncation at that budget, retrieval differed by +29.0 points [+24.7, +33.3], summaries by +5.0 [+2.4, +7.7] and compression by +2.4 [$-$0.4, +5.3] (Table~\ref{tab:p5-secondary}); compression (+2.4 points [$-$0.4, +5.3]) was not separated from truncation at that budget, and the alternatives were not tested against each other. At 4,096 tokens, BM25 ranking of past exchanges was not separated from MiniLM ranking (+2.2 points [$-$1.7, +6.1]), and compressing only the recent window instead of the whole history changed compression accuracy by $-$5.2 points [$-$9.7, $-$0.7]. Equal budgets did not give equal prompt lengths: at 1,024 tokens retrieval delivered 756 prompt tokens on average and compression 1,022, because retrieval stops at the first ranked round that does not fit (Figure~\ref{fig:p5-cost}). These comparisons are descriptive, and a contrast that is significant at one budget but not at the other does not by itself establish an interaction between policy and budget.

\newcommand{\PfiveArchiveTransfer}{\label{sec:transfer}
Two descriptive checks repeat the comparison outside the primary setting, each at 4,096 tokens. The first gives the same 500 questions and session summaries to Qwen3-8B, a smaller attention-only model from the same developer, served by llama.cpp with its own tokenizer and chat template (Table~\ref{tab:p5-crossmodel}). Retrieval again had the highest point estimate (64.2\%), and the four point estimates kept the order of the primary 27B run (M, S, C, T). This check changes the model and the serving stack together, so it isolates neither.

The second check uses 200 LoCoMo questions. A pre-specified rule chose M from S, M and C as the alternative with the most correct answers on the primary LongMemEval results; ties use that order, and selection does not require beating T. Because the rule selected retrieval, this check does not repeat the truncation-versus-summarization comparison that Kyrkewood ran on LoCoMo \citep{kyrkewood2026sleeping}. Retrieval was judged correct on 68.0\% of the questions against 17.0\% for truncation, a difference of +51.0 points (pointwise Newcombe interval [+42.3, +58.4], unadjusted McNemar $p=\ensuremath{1.71\times10^{-23}}$); these question-level calculations ignore that questions share conversation histories. Token F1 compares the whole visible answer with a short gold string, so every extra word lowers it and its level is low for both arms; it changed by +4.5 points, with a conversation-resampled 95\% interval of [+3.5, +5.7] over only 10 conversations (Table~\ref{tab:p5-locomo}). Neither result establishes general superiority across datasets.

\newcommand{\PfiveArchiveScorers}{\label{sec:scorers}
Scorer agreement is not validation against human correctness, and identical failure labels can inflate it, so the agreement rates below use only successful generations with a nonempty verdict from each compared scorer. No generation or verdict failed in any 4,096-token cell of the three runs, so the rule that scores failures as incorrect changed no accuracy. In the 27B run the local judge agreed with the generator acting as a second judge on 96.6--98.2\% of verdicts per policy ($\kappa$ 0.90--0.95), and with lexical containment of the gold answer on 78.0--91.4\% of short-answer items (Table~\ref{tab:p5-scorers}). In the 8B run the second judge, Qwen3-8B judging its own answers, agreed with the local judge on 69.4--87.2\% of verdicts ($\kappa$ 0.28--0.70); it enters no reported accuracy. In the 27B run the two judges' largest disagreement, 3.4\% of one policy's verdicts, is less than half the smallest gap between adjacent policies at 4,096 tokens (12.2 points), so scoring with the second judge could not have changed the order of the four policies; scored by lexical containment on the 440 short-answer items, the order was also unchanged (retrieval 55.7\%, summaries 34.5\%, compression 22.0\% and truncation 10.2\%). No agreement rate shows which scorer is correct. Every nonempty verdict in the displayed cells was a bare yes or no (allowing a final period), and no empty visible answer was credited; the upstream rule that credits any reply containing ``yes'' was kept unchanged.

\newcommand{\PfiveArchiveCost}{\label{sec:cost}
Figure~\ref{fig:p5-cost} shows the prompt tokens each policy delivered and the seconds recorded per question. At 4,096 tokens the four policies delivered 3,559--3,993 prompt tokens on average. Mean generation time per answer was 15.0\,s for truncation, 17.8\,s for retrieval, 19.2\,s for summaries and 24.9\,s for compression. Recorded selection added 0.19\,s for truncation, 0.42\,s for compression and 4.37\,s for retrieval, whose timer includes computing embeddings not already cached; summaries had no per-question selection timer. These timings follow policy-specific accounting boundaries and exclude summary generation, classifier preprocessing and the pause between requests (Section~\ref{sec:statistics}), so they do not rank the policies by end-to-end cost.

\newcommand{\PfiveArchiveMechanisms}{\paragraph{Possible mechanisms.} The design does not isolate why each policy lost evidence; the following explanations are consistent with the diagnostics but untested. LongMemEval-S spreads answer-bearing sessions throughout each history, so a recency window seldom reaches them: at 4,096 tokens truncation kept 17.7 of 493.5 messages on average. Session summaries reach further back (17.1 of 47.7 sessions) but were written before the question was known and are short, so a covered session can still lose the needed fact; their coverage of evidence sessions (44.0\%) bounds what they can supply from above. Whole-history compression runs far outside the range in which LLMLingua-2 was validated (Section~\ref{sec:policies}) and kept 26.3\% of the evidence words. Compressing only the recent window, inside that range, changed accuracy by $-$5.2 points [$-$9.7, $-$0.7] even though it kept more of the evidence words (37.3\%); word recall counts lexical overlap anywhere in the window, which may still omit the answer-bearing sessions. On abstention questions, more history may give the model material to answer from when it should decline, which would explain why truncation was credited on 30 of 30 and the other policies on 24--25.

\newcommand{\PfiveArchiveServing}{
\label{sec:serving}
The generator is Qwen3.8-27B \citep{qwen2026qwen38card}, a hybrid model whose
64 layers are 48 Gated DeltaNet layers, which carry a recurrent state, and 16
attention layers. It is quantized to 2.00 bits per weight in the EXL3 trellis
format \citep{turboderp2026qwen38exl3}, with its own multi-token-prediction
(MTP) draft head, and runs on one RTX 4080 Laptop GPU (12\,GB), served by
TabbyAPI \citep{theroyallab2025tabbyapi} on exllamav3 1.4.7
\citep{turboderp2025exllamav3}. This is the configuration the author uses for
interactive work on this card: fully resident on the GPU, with a 32,768-token
context and speculative decoding from the MTP head. The engine is the official
wheel with the author's recorded source patches. One sets how often the
recurrent state is checkpointed during prefill, which the launcher set to every
1,024 tokens. Another adds opt-in switches for a draft-acceptance probe and for
standard speculative sampling; the launcher did not set them, although the
server's runtime environment was not recorded. By the frozen code, neither
switch changes the sampled distribution, and the default MTP path samples the
target model and accepts only draft tokens that match.

Thinking is on: the model reasons in its own thinking block, at the template's
\q{medium} reasoning effort, chosen because the template's default effort
(\q{xhigh}) injects a system message. Requests specified temperature 0.2 and
top-p 0.95, as stored with every request; other samplers kept TabbyAPI's
defaults, and with the server's parameter log off, the applied values were not
read back (Table~\ref{tab:p5-envelope}). The completion cap was \PfiveCap{}
tokens, fixed by the pre-registered pilot rule (16,384 tokens if any pilot
completion reached 6,144, otherwise 8,192), with a 900-second timeout. TabbyAPI
accepts no per-request seed, so sampled requests are not byte-reproducible
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

}

\newcommand{\PfiveArchivePrompts}{
Every arm uses the LongMemEval reader template, the prompt the benchmark gives
its answering model (the upstream code's ``history chats'' prompt without
chain-of-thought instructions), with session blocks carrying their dates, one
user message and no system prompt. The harness renders the prompt with the
model's chat template and sends it as a raw completion. The budget $B$ counts
every token of that string with the served tokenizer, and before each request
the server's own token count had to equal the harness count; it did for every
request. Every arm fills toward $B$ from its own material, and the delivered
tokens are reported per arm. Both budgets lie far below the served
32,768-token context: the study measures short prompts by design, and the
larger budget equals Ollama's default context on GPUs with less than 24\,GiB of
memory \citep{ollama_chatprompt}.

\newcommand{\PfiveArchivePolicies}{
\label{sec:policies}
\begin{description}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item[T, recency truncation.] The oldest whole messages are dropped until the
  prompt fits, keeping each surviving message's session header. This is the
  prompt-side behavior of Ollama's message truncation \citep{ollama_chatprompt}.
\item[S, session summaries.] The generator summarizes each session that S
  needs to fill $B = 4{,}096$, once and newest first, with thinking off, greedy
  decoding, at most 200 tokens and a prompt that keeps user facts,
  assistant-provided specifics and every date expression. Of the 8,350
  summaries, 5,819 stopped at the 200-token cap. Summaries are cached and
  question-agnostic. S fills $B$ with whole summaries, newest first, shown in
  chronological order.
\item[C, LLMLingua-2 over the whole history.] Keep-probabilities come from the
  library's own chunking and classifier \citep{pan2024llmlingua2}; the
  library's per-chunk keep rule is applied at the largest attainable keep rate
  that fits, and a self-test confirmed byte identity with the library's
  \q{compress\_prompt}. When even the finest rate overflows, which happens at
  1,024 tokens in the prepared prompts, the oldest compressed chunks are
  dropped. The required compression is about 30-fold at 4,096 tokens and about
  120-fold at 1,024, far beyond the 2--5-fold range in which LLMLingua-2 was
  validated.
\item[M, retrieval from history.] A user turn and its immediately following
  assistant turn form a round; other turns form single-turn rounds. All
  rounds in the history are candidates, including recent ones. MiniLM-L6
  embeddings \citep{wang2020minilm,reimers2019sbert} of the question text and of
  each round, truncated at 256 word pieces, rank the rounds by cosine
  similarity, breaking ties toward recency. The largest fitting prefix of that
  ranking is shown chronologically under its session headers. No separate
  recent-message buffer is reserved, and a round that does not fit is not
  skipped to try smaller, lower-ranked rounds. The letter M is the
  pre-registration's label for this memory arm.
\item[Controls and variants.] O shows the oracle evidence sessions in full,
  only for the \PfiveOFitN{} items whose prompt fits 4,096 tokens (the fitting
  items). E shows no history. Cw compresses only a recent window of about four
  times $B$ (\PfDiagWindowTokensCwOne{} and \PfDiagWindowTokensCwFour{} tokens on
  average at the two budgets), which keeps the compression inside the validated
  range, and Mbm25 replaces the embedder with BM25 \citep{robertson2009bm25}.
\end{description}



}

\newcommand{\PfiveArchiveScoring}{
The primary score follows LongMemEval's judge protocol
\citep{wu2025longmemeval}: the upstream per-type judge prompts, verbatim, and
the upstream rule that counts an answer as correct when the judge's reply
contains ``yes''. The official judge is a hosted GPT-4o snapshot, and no hosted
credential was available; the judge here is gpt-oss-20b
\citep{openai2025gptoss}, a local model from a different developer than the
generator, served by llama.cpp \citep{gerganov2023llamacpp} at temperature 0
with low reasoning effort. The judge sees only the generator's visible answer,
its reply with the thinking block removed; an answer whose thinking block never
closed has an empty visible answer, which the judge scores like any other. Two
secondary scorers provide agreement checks, reported as raw agreement and
Cohen's $\kappa$ \citep{cohen1960kappa}: the generator judging with the same
prompts, and normalized containment of the gold answer on the short-answer
items (all but the preference and abstention questions).

}

\newcommand{\PfiveArchiveStatistics}{
\label{sec:statistics}
The confirmatory family is the three paired comparisons of S, C and M against
T at 4,096 tokens, each an exact two-sided McNemar test \citep{mcnemar1947} on
the 500 items, with Holm correction \citep{holm1979} at a family-wise 0.05. An
explicitly failed generation counts as incorrect, and the analysis does not run
while any request is missing. Differences carry Newcombe method-10 intervals
\citep{newcombe1998paired} and accuracies Wilson intervals \citep{wilson1927}.
Everything else is descriptive with pointwise intervals: the 1,024-token
contrasts, the variants, the ranking across budgets, the oracle gap, question
types, evidence retention, cost, and the LoCoMo and cross-model checks.

The cross-model check rebuilds T, S, C and M at 4,096 tokens with Qwen3-8B's
own tokenizer and chat template. It uses the primary generator's session
summaries, including the few older sessions that only the 8B tokenizer's budget
reaches (Appendix~\ref{app:execution}). Its llama.cpp requests apply the
recorded seed; the primary TabbyAPI requests cannot. For LoCoMo, the alternative
to T is the highest-scoring S, M or C arm on the completed primary judge labels,
with ties broken S, M, C. Token-F1 intervals resample whole conversations 10,000
times (seed 20260920), recomputing the item-weighted mean in each sample.
Question-level accuracy intervals and tests remain descriptive; shared
conversations limit their interpretation.

Evidence diagnostics use different denominators: truncation and retrieval
report the share of evidence turns retained, summarization reports the share of
evidence-bearing sessions summarized, and compression reports evidence-word
recall, the share of distinct words of four or more characters in the evidence
turns that appear anywhere in the compressed history. These are plotted
separately. Summarizing an evidence-bearing session does not establish that its
relevant facts survive the summary, and word overlap does not establish that an
answer can be recovered.

Each evidence diagnostic is averaged across questions with a defined
denominator; questions with an undefined fraction are omitted, not scored zero.
The turn-retention means for truncation and retrieval each use 479 questions.

Timing coverage differs by policy. T records selection and token counting;
C records selection over previously computed classifier scores, excluding
that classifier preprocessing. S has no per-question selection timer, and
summary generation is separate. M's timer includes ranking, token counting
and embeddings not already in its in-process cache; the ranking time is
recorded once and attached to both primary-budget records. Generation seconds
include prefill, which the server shortens when it reuses cached prompt pages
(Table~\ref{tab:p5-envelope}). The displayed costs are therefore neither an
end-to-end comparison nor a clean warm-cache latency comparison. Missing timing
is not treated as zero.

}

\newcommand{\PfiveArchiveLimitations}{


\paragraph{Scoring.} The primary judge is a local model, gpt-oss-20b, not the
hosted GPT-4o judge of the official protocol, and no answer was checked by a
human. Agreement with a second local judge or with a lexical rule shows
consistency, not correctness, and the upstream rule that credits any reply
containing ``yes'' was kept.

\paragraph{Implementations and budgets.} Each policy is one implementation with
fixed choices: question-agnostic summaries capped at 200 tokens, LLMLingua-2
run far outside its originally studied compression range, and retrieval that
does not skip a round that fails to fit. Other summary prompts, compressors,
rankers or budgets could rank differently. Equal budgets do not equalize
delivered length or evidence. Summaries can omit or distort facts, and neither
a covered source session nor retained evidence words show that the needed fact
survived.

\paragraph{Generality.} The confirmatory evidence comes from one 27B generator
quantized to 2.00 bits per weight, on one GPU, with sampled answers. The
questions ask about facts that LongMemEval-S places throughout long histories;
continuity within the current session, which recency serves directly, was not
tested. The Qwen3-8B check changes the model and the serving setup together; it
does not isolate architecture or establish cross-family generality. The LoCoMo
questions come from only a few conversations, so its question-level tests ignore
shared histories and its resampled interval rests on few clusters. Only the
three contrasts with truncation at 4,096 tokens are confirmatory; every other
comparison, including the order among the alternatives, is descriptive. The
oracle arm covers a selected subset of \PfiveOFitN{} questions and is not a
full-context upper bound over all 500.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and environment.} TabbyAPI accepts no per-request
seed, so the recorded seed was never applied: exact analysis reproduction relies
on the saved responses, and a rerun cannot be expected to reproduce sampled
answers byte for byte. Grading ran a month after generation, in the updated
environment described in Appendix~\ref{app:execution}. The judges read saved
answers, so the answers themselves could not change, but both judges' verdicts
were produced in that later environment.

\paragraph{Cost.} Recorded timings omit offline preparation (session summaries,
classifier passes, embeddings) and its reuse across questions, follow
policy-specific accounting boundaries, and exclude request pacing, which affects
total elapsed cost and possibly thermal conditions. These measurements cannot
identify a universally best memory system or rank the policies by end-to-end
cost.

}

\newcommand{\PfiveArchiveRelated}{The Sleeping Agent \citep{kyrkewood2026sleeping} compared truncation and
rolling summarization at about 4K tokens on LoCoMo with hosted models and found
truncation behind summarization in judged accuracy (0.171 against 0.238), with
date loss in the summaries as the main failure. Our LoCoMo check compares

summarization contrast only if summaries are selected. Retain or Consolidate

Sentence-level retrieval at a fixed budget matched full-history accuracy with

contrast was not tested. Bei et al.\ \citep{bei2026compression} compared a

\paragraph{Learned compression.}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
When chat history overflows a local model's prompt, keeping recent messages can
discard answers that retrieval preserves. We studied a \PfiveBits{}-bit
Qwen3.8-27B on a \PfiveGpuMemory{}\,GB laptop GPU using \PfNMFour{}
LongMemEval-S questions with histories of roughly \PfiveHistoryTokens{} tokens.
Four policies shared an enforced \PfiveLargeBudget{}-token prompt limit; a local
judge scored the answers, with pre-registered paired tests and Holm correction
against recency truncation. Retrieval was judged correct on \PfCorrectMFour{}
questions (\PfAccMFour\%) against \PfCorrectTFour{} (\PfAccTFour\%) for
truncation: \PfiveRetrievalRatio{} times as many answers, a paired difference of
\PfDiffMvT{} points (\PfiveConfidence\% interval
[\PfDiffLoMvT, \PfDiffHiMvT]). Given only the evidence sessions, the model
was judged correct on \PfCorrectOFour{} of the \PfNOFour{} fitting questions
(\PfAccOFour\%). The descriptive comparison did not resolve
an accuracy difference between BM25 and embedding retrieval. These
results favor retrieving across the history when a local assistant must answer
questions about earlier sessions. They cover the tested implementations,
primary model, benchmark and local judge; the selected oracle subset also
removes distractors, and the study does not rank policies by end-to-end cost.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Retrieval yielded \PfiveRetrievalRatio{} times as many judged-correct answers
as recency truncation when a local Qwen3.8-27B answered LongMemEval-S questions
within a \PfiveLargeBudget{}-token prompt (Figure~\ref{fig:p5-gap}). The
comparison holds the answering model and prompt limit fixed: the main change
is which past exchanges reach the model. For an operator choosing how to fill
a short prompt, that choice produced a large difference in judged answer accuracy.

A conversation can span many sessions while the prompt carries only a small
part of it. Keeping recent messages preserves continuity, but a question about
an earlier session may need an older exchange. Summaries and learned
compression fit more source material by transforming it; retrieval instead
selects exchanges using the question. We compare these standard choices on
full histories, under the same limit counted by the answering model's
tokenizer, including instructions and template tokens.

CoRA-Bench already compares truncation, retrieval and learned compression on
full LongMemEval histories with a hosted reader and larger selected-context
budgets \citep{pardawala2026cora}. LiveCtx uses a hosted reader and a character
budget \citep{liu2026livectx}. The question here is narrower: how much answer
accuracy these implementations preserve for a quantized local reader within a
short total prompt. This is a measurement of existing policies, with a
session-summary comparison, rather than a new memory method.

The retrieval gap leads Section~\ref{sec:results}. Two supporting comparisons
help interpret it: the model answered nearly every fitting oracle question
when shown only its evidence, and keyword retrieval was not separated from
embedding retrieval. Section~\ref{sec:setup} defines the comparison and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} gives its practical implication. Appendix~\ref{app:nulls}
keeps the complete policy grid, smaller-budget and transfer checks, scorer
details and limitations.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Data and answering model}
The primary corpus is cleaned LongMemEval-S \citep{wu2025longmemeval}:
\PfNMFour{} questions about user--assistant histories of roughly
\PfiveHistoryTokens{} tokens, with annotated evidence turns and questions dated
after the history. The history scale uses a characters/4 estimate, rather than
a served-tokenizer minimum. The same questions, evidence, dates and gold answers are
used across policies. The oracle uses the benchmark's evidence-only sessions,
with its question date aligned to the cleaned corpus before primary generation;
only the \PfiveOFitN{} complete oracle prompts that fit are evaluated.

The reader is Qwen3.8-27B \citep{qwen2026qwen38card}, quantized to
\PfiveBits{} bits per weight in EXL3 format \citep{turboderp2026qwen38exl3}.
It runs fully on an RTX 4080 Laptop GPU with \PfiveGpuMemory{}\,GB memory,
using TabbyAPI on exllamav3 \citep{theroyallab2025tabbyapi,turboderp2025exllamav3},
with its multi-token-prediction draft head. The served context is
\PfiveContext{} tokens; the short prompt limits are experimental constraints.
Table~\ref{tab:p5-envelope} records the hardware, exact revisions, cache state,
requested settings and gaps in the runtime records.

\subsection{Prompts and history selection}
Each policy uses the LongMemEval reader template: dated session blocks, one
user message and no system prompt. The harness renders the model's chat
template, counts the entire prompt with the served tokenizer, and requires the
server's count to agree before sending it. Instructions, question, dates and
template tokens all consume the limit. Equal limits do not require identical
delivered lengths: indivisible messages or rounds can leave unused space.

Recency truncation removes the oldest whole messages until the prompt fits,
keeping the surviving session headers, as in Ollama's prompt-side truncation
\citep{ollama_chatprompt}. Retrieval considers rounds from the whole history:
a user turn and its immediately following assistant turn form a round, with
other turns treated singly. MiniLM-L6 embeddings
\citep{wang2020minilm,reimers2019sbert} rank rounds by cosine similarity to the
question, breaking ties toward recency. Each embedding input is truncated to
\PfiveEmbeddingTokens{} word pieces. The largest fitting prefix of the ranking
is rendered chronologically; there is no reserved recent-message buffer, and
the selector stops at the first round that does not fit.

BM25 \citep{robertson2009bm25} replaces only the ranker. The oracle instead
shows the full annotated evidence sessions, and a no-history control removes
the conversation. The other primary policies concatenate question-agnostic
session summaries or apply LLMLingua-2 to the full history
\citep{pan2024llmlingua2}. Their detailed construction, compression variant,
and the \PfiveSmallBudget{}-token comparison are retained in
Appendix~\ref{app:policy-details}.

\subsection{Generation and scoring}
Requests set temperature \PfiveTemperature{}, top-p \PfiveTopP{} and a fixed
\PfiveCap{}-token completion cap, with medium reasoning effort and a
\PfiveTimeout{}-second timeout. Other samplers retain the server defaults.
TabbyAPI accepts no per-request seed, so a rerun need not reproduce sampled
answers byte for byte. Requests run serially with a pause between them.
Prompts are frozen and hash-listed before generation; completed requests are
not resubmitted. Failed generation or judging is scored incorrect, while a
missing request prevents analysis. The one excluded response and the dated
environment changes are recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:execution}.

The primary judge is local gpt-oss-20b \citep{openai2025gptoss}, served by
llama.cpp \citep{gerganov2023llamacpp}, using LongMemEval's per-type judge
prompts and its rule that a reply containing ``yes'' counts as correct. It
sees only the visible answer after removal of the thinking block. An
unterminated block therefore leaves an empty answer. The benchmark's official
hosted judge is replaced by this local judge; no answer received human
correctness review. Appendix~\ref{sec:scorers} reports the secondary judge and
lexical agreement checks.

\subsection{Paired analysis}
The pre-registered confirmatory family compares summaries, compression and
retrieval separately with truncation at \PfiveLargeBudget{} tokens, on the
same \PfNMFour{} items. Each contrast uses an exact two-sided McNemar test
\citep{mcnemar1947}, with Holm correction \citep{holm1979} at family-wise
\PfiveAlpha{}. Accuracy intervals use Wilson's method \citep{wilson1927};
paired difference intervals use Newcombe method 10 \citep{newcombe1998paired}.
Intervals are pointwise \PfiveConfidence\% intervals. BM25, oracle, the smaller
budget, subgroups, transfer checks and comparisons among alternatives are
descriptive and lie outside that confirmatory family. All result values below
come from the frozen analysis.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The retrieval gap}
\label{sec:gap}
At the same prompt limit, retrieval yielded \PfCorrectMFour{} judged-correct
answers and truncation \PfCorrectTFour{} (Figure~\ref{fig:p5-gap}). The
paired difference was \PfDiffMvT{} percentage points, with a Newcombe interval
of [\PfDiffLoMvT, \PfDiffHiMvT]. Of the discordant pairs, retrieval alone was
correct on \PfArmOnlyMvT{} questions and truncation alone on
\PfRefOnlyMvT{}. The lead survived Holm correction across the full
pre-registered family (adjusted $p=\PfHolmMvT$).

Across the 479 questions with annotated evidence turns, the mean per-question
fraction retained was \PfDiagEvidenceTurnsKeptMFour\% for retrieval and
\PfDiagEvidenceTurnsKeptTFour\% for truncation. Both means use the same eligible
questions. The much larger retained share is consistent with the accuracy
gap: questions about earlier sessions often require text outside the recent
window. The complete policy scores, failures, uncertainty and all three
confirmatory contrasts remain in Table~\ref{tab:p5-grid} and
Table~\ref{tab:p5-primary}.

\subsection{Evidence-only answers}
\label{sec:oracle-main}
Shown only the evidence sessions, the same model was judged correct on
\PfCorrectOFour{} of \PfNOFour{} fitting questions: \PfAccOFour\%, with a
Wilson interval of [\PfAccLoOFour, \PfAccHiOFour]\%. On those same questions,
the oracle led retrieval by \PfOracleGapM{} points and truncation by
\PfOracleGapT{} (Table~\ref{tab:p5-oracle}). This shows that the local model
could read the supplied evidence for nearly every question in this selected
subset. It supports a role for history selection in the lost answers. The
oracle also removes the other history, so it does not isolate omitted evidence
from distraction or provide a ceiling for the full benchmark.

Retrieval's advantage also occurred with BM25; the descriptive comparison did
not resolve a difference between BM25 and MiniLM (Appendix~\ref{sec:budget}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The main result changes a concrete starting point for a local assistant:
when the task is answering questions about earlier sessions, retrieve across
the history before treating a recent-message window as the default. The model
and token limit stayed fixed while the selected history changed. On this
workload, a recency baseline supplied little annotated evidence, whereas
retrieval supplied much more and yielded far more judged-correct answers.

The BM25 comparison narrows a second engineering decision: these measurements
do not resolve an accuracy benefit from replacing lexical ranking with MiniLM.
The evidence-only comparison identifies a useful follow-up question: how much
of the remaining loss comes from omitted evidence and how much from distracting
text? Separately varying those inputs would distinguish the two explanations.

For memory evaluations on histories with evidence spread across sessions,
retrieval is a more informative comparator than recency truncation alone.
Beating a recent window leaves open whether a new method improves on selecting
relevant exchanges. The complete secondary comparisons, implementation limits
and incomplete cost accounting are kept in Appendix~\ref{app:nulls}, so they
remain available without extending the main result beyond its evidence.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
CoRA-Bench \citep{pardawala2026cora} is the closest comparison. It evaluates
truncation, lexical and dense retrieval, reranking and learned compression on
full LongMemEval-S histories. Its reader is hosted Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite, its
budgets count selected context rather than the entire served prompt, and it has
no session-summary arm. LiveCtx \citep{liu2026livectx} compares recent context,
retrieval and compression with hosted qwen3-max under a character-count budget.
Both make evidence selection a focus of evaluation; the
contribution here is the measured size of the difference for a quantized local
reader under a short total prompt-token limit.

BudgetBench \citep{rao2026budgetbench} studies fixed per-call budgets, but its
LongMemEval comparison uses evidence-only histories and a hosted reader.
WhenLoss \citep{yu2026whenloss} compares memory systems with a larger
truncation budget. These differences matter because budget matching must state
both what is counted and what history is available. RENDER
\citep{si2026render} shows that evidence format alone can change answers;
all policies here share one rendering template.

CarryOn \citep{li2026carryon} includes Qwen3.8-27B among active agents using
context-management methods, on AppWorld, OfficeBench and Tau2 Telecom. Those
agent tasks differ from the static conversational questions studied here.
LLMLingua-2 \citep{pan2024llmlingua2} supplies the learned compression baseline;
its tested compression regime and the summary comparisons in other work are
discussed with the corresponding limits in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-related}.

\section{Artifact availability and reproducibility}
\label{sec:artifacts}
The release is being prepared and has no published artifact identifier.
The analysis-only component contains sanitized scored records, aggregate
results and pinned code to regenerate the primary and cross-model statistics,
tables and figures. It replays archived scores; it does not rescore answer
correctness or prove original execution. Conversation, question, answer and
judge text are not redistributed. LoCoMo is aggregate-only, so its token-F1
conversation bootstrap cannot be regenerated publicly. A full rerun requires
the pinned upstream data, models and serving configuration in
Table~\ref{tab:p5-envelope} and generates new sampled answers. LongMemEval is
MIT-licensed and LoCoMo is CC BY-NC 4.0. Appendix~\ref{app:execution} preserves
the execution record.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
With this local Qwen3.8-27B and a \PfiveLargeBudget{}-token prompt limit, retrieval
produced more judged-correct LongMemEval-S answers than recency truncation. Successful evidence-only answers on the fitting subset support
better history selection as a practical route to improvement, and the
descriptive ranker comparison leaves BM25 as a credible starting baseline.
The usable lesson is to retrieve earlier exchanges when the question requires
them; Appendix~\ref{app:nulls} gives the complete results and limits.
